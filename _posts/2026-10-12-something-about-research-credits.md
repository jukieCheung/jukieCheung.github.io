*Some scattered thoughts on authorship, open source, AI, and academic recognition.*

##An Unexpected NeurIPS Paper

This April, I went to Tokyo for a Sakurazaka46 concert. While I was there, I caught up with an old undergraduate friend who is now studying at the University of Tokyo. He brought along one of his classmates, and the three of us had dinner together.

We talked about random things, had a good time, and exchanged contacts. But a few months later, I saw a NeurIPS paper on his classmate's WeChat.

What caught my attention was that he hadn't been directly involved in the research presented in the paper. Another group had used one of his GitHub projects and included him as a co-author.

I found this rather interesting. Not because I thought there was anything wrong with it. On the contrary, I thought it was quite nice that an open-source contribution could receive that kind of recognition.

But it did leave me wondering what exactly counts as a contribution worthy of authorship?

## The Credit Assignment Problem

Recently, I read [Toru's post on the future of academia](https://toruowo.github.io/blog/posts/2026-09-18-what-next-for-academia.html), which raised a related question about AI agents conducting research.

Suppose an AI agent reads ten papers, clones several repositories, adapts existing algorithms, and eventually produces a new result.

The person who prompted the agent; the developers of the agent; the researchers behind the algorithms and repositories? Or everyone involved, who deserves the credit?

Of course, if I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants, as Issac Newton said. That's the whole point of cumulative science.

But historically, we have at least tried to preserve some record of intellectual lineage through citations and authorship. AI makes this considerably more complicated. An agent may integrate ideas from dozens of sources without producing a transparent account of where each idea came from.

And yet, interestingly, the Tokyo story suggests that our credit assignment system was already rather ambiguous, even before autonomous research agents entered the picture. We have never had a universally convincing way to translate intellectual contribution into academic recognition.

AI might simply make that ambiguity impossible to ignore.

## The Strange Economy of Academic Recognition

There is another thing I've been noticing lately. Academic self-promotion seems to be becoming an increasingly important part of being a researcher, particularly in AI.

Every day, my timeline is full of papers described as *exciting*, *groundbreaking*, or *a game changer*. There are beautiful project pages, carefully prepared figures, impressive demonstrations, and threads explaining why a particular result matters.

But I wonder how much of academic recognition comes from the contribution itself, and how much comes from the ability to make that contribution visible. The distinction becomes even more uncomfortable when considering the unequal starting points of researchers.

A PhD student at a famous laboratory may have immediate access to an audience of thousands, simply because of their institution, supervisor, or collaborators. Another researcher doing equally interesting work might struggle to get anyone to notice.

None of this means that well-connected researchers are less capable, or that promoting one's work is somehow dishonest.

It just means that **the distribution of attention is not necessarily the distribution of scientific merit.**

And in a system where attention can lead to citations, collaborations, internships, and job opportunities, this distinction matters.

Perhaps this is why I sometimes feel that doing research is no longer quite enough. One must also make sure that everyone knows one is doing research.

A rather strange additional responsibility that makes everybody anxiety, innit?

## What Are We Actually Rewarding?

Coming back to that unexpected NeurIPS authorship, I think what interests me most is not whether the developer deserved it. He may well have. What interests me is how different forms of contribution receive radically different forms of recognition.

Now AI agents are entering this already complicated system, making it easier to combine existing knowledge into new results while potentially making the underlying contributions harder to trace.

This reminded me of [Phillip Isola's recent essay](https://web.mit.edu/phillipi/www/writing/PhD-in-age-of-AI.html), On the Value of Doing a PhD in the Age of AI.

In his third point, Isola acknowledges that we need to rethink credit assignment. As AI models absorb and build upon the work of countless researchers, he suggests that we can take pride in contributing to something larger than ourselves. Ultimately, the achievements of frontier AI should belong to humanity as a whole.

I find this perspective both admirable and somewhat unsettling. I agree with the ideal. Science is a collective endeavor, and perhaps we should be less obsessed with attaching individual names to every incremental advance.

But there is a rather practical problem. Collective scientific progress does not mean collective access to academic opportunities. For PhD students outside elite universities and top laboratories, credit is not merely about recognition or personal pride. It is often one of the few things we can accumulate to improve our circumstances.

We need publications, citations, and visible contributions to compete for internships, research positions, postdoctoral appointments, and eventually faculty jobs. We need people to know what we have done, because we cannot always rely on the reputation of our institutions or the networks of our supervisors to speak for us. And even within the existing system, the playing field is hardly level.

Now imagine a future in which AI systems can absorb our methods, reuse our code, build upon our ideas, and produce results that receive far more attention than the original contributions.

We are told that we should be happy to have contributed to the progress of science. And perhaps we should.

But pride does not appear on a CV in quite the same way as a publication. It does not necessarily get you shortlisted for a postdoc, secure a research grant, or convince a hiring committee to take a chance on someone from an unfamiliar institution.

For someone still trying to establish a research career, losing the ability to claim credit for their work could mean losing the opportunity to remain in research at all. If the connection between contribution and recognition becomes weaker, who will bear the cost?

My worry is that it will not be distributed equally.

## A Rather Awkward Conclusion

I don't think authorship, citations, or self-promotion are inherently bad. Nor do I think every dependency should automatically entitle its creator to co-authorship.

And I certainly don't have a better system ready to replace the existing one.

Anyway, these are just some scattered thoughts. I have no grand proposal for redesigning academia.

I just a PhD student, not even a candidate. I still want to do good research, and I still hope that good research will speak for itself.

Though, apparently, it might also need a GitHub repository, a polished project page, and a Twitter thread.
